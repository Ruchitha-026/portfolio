Artifact 1

Artifact 1 contains two distinct but complementary works. Artifact 1A communicates AI history through interactive visual design. Artifact 1B demonstrates design thinking, custom-GPT development, testing, and responsible AI use.

Artifact 1A

The Intelligence Line: Interactive AI Metro Timeline

Purpose and audience

The timeline was created for classmates, instructors, recruiters, and professionals who need a concise but meaningful overview of AI development. The metro-map metaphor makes a complex history easier to navigate by turning major periods into connected “stations.”

Skills demonstrated

Historical research, audience-centered communication, source attribution, information hierarchy, responsive HTML/CSS, interactive JavaScript, accessibility features, and visual storytelling.

Interactive preview: select any station to expand its details.Open live GitHub page ↗

Artifact 1A Reflection

Creating this timeline helped me understand that a professional artifact should do more than repeat information. It should organize information for a specific audience and make the main ideas easy to explore. I selected a metro-line format because AI history includes periods of rapid progress, pauses, renewed investment, and important transfer points. The connected stations visually communicate that AI did not develop through one straight technical path.

The strongest part of this artifact is the combination of research and interaction. Each milestone includes a date, key people or organizations, a short explanation, and the historical impact. The expandable station design keeps the page from overwhelming the viewer while still making detailed information available. I also learned to consider responsive design, keyboard-friendly buttons, readable contrast, reduced-motion preferences, and source transparency.

If I improve this artifact, I would add category filters, a searchable milestone index, and a comparison mode showing how changes in data, computing power, algorithms, and public expectations influenced each stage. Professionally, this project demonstrates that I can translate complex technical history into an organized and audience-friendly digital experience.

Artifact 1B

EventEase AI: Responsible Student Event-Planning Assistant

Problem addressed

Student organizations may have a strong event idea but struggle with approvals, venues, budgets, accessibility, technology, role assignments, and backup plans. EventEase AI turns these connected requirements into a manageable planning workflow.

Professional relevance

The project demonstrates requirements gathering, risk identification, boundary definition, scenario testing, documentation, adaptation to change, accessibility awareness, and ethical decision-making—all skills that connect strongly with quality and validation work.

Interactive design-thinking process

The users are student organizers who may be balancing coursework, limited budgets, approvals, accessibility needs, and last-minute changes.

Testing evidence

Key results

The normal planning scenario produced a three-week plan with an estimated total of $275, a $30 contingency reserve, and a $25 cushion. When the budget dropped from $500 to $200, the assistant preserved the central educational activity and reduced lower-priority expenses first. When a venue became unavailable, it recommended an approved alternative room, virtual delivery, postponement, or cancellation rather than an unsafe or unapproved option. It also refused requests for forged approvals and receipts.

Artifact 1B Reflection

This AI Lab changed my understanding of what makes an AI assistant useful. A strong assistant is not defined by how much content it can generate. It is defined by whether it has a clear purpose, collects the right information, respects boundaries, adapts to changing conditions, and makes its assumptions visible.

EventEase AI was effective because the design-thinking process kept the tool centered on actual student needs. The tests showed that it could preserve the main purpose of an event while responding to budget reductions, venue problems, and scheduling constraints. The honesty test was especially important because it demonstrated that the assistant should refuse deceptive documents while still offering legitimate alternatives.

The project also connects to my validation background. I defined expected behavior, created test scenarios, compared observed results with expectations, documented limitations, and identified improvements. Future versions could include a quick-plan mode, reusable checklists by event size, clearer decision gates, and progress tracking. The central lesson is that AI-generated plans remain starting points; human organizers must verify policies, approvals, prices, accessibility arrangements, and final decisions.
